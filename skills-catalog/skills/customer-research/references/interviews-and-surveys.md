# Interviews and surveys

Asking customers directly is the highest-signal research there is. Your part is preparation and analysis: shortlist who to ask, draft the invitation, write the questions, and analyse the notes or transcript afterwards. The Commander sends every message and runs every call. Nothing here is sent by you.

## Ground rules

**Keep it casual.** Once a customer feels they are in "a research study" they give the polished, acceptable answer. Frame it as a chat. The Commander should not lead, pitch or defend the product; the job is to listen.

**Try to prove the assumption wrong.** Research is for disproving beliefs, not confirming them. Write questions that can return an answer nobody wants to hear. If no question could prove the current belief wrong, rewrite the questions.

## Watch before you ask (Sales Safari, from Amy Hoy)

Before any interview, observe the audience where it already gathers, without interrupting. Read threads, reviews and comments for four things:

| Look for | What it is |
|---|---|
| Pains | Problems, frustrations and workarounds they describe unprompted |
| Jargon | Their exact words and shorthand |
| Recommendations | What they tell each other to buy, try or avoid |
| Worldview | Beliefs and biases; how they see themselves and the problem |

This is the passive half of the work and it tells you what to ask and in whose words. The source guides cover where and how.

## Who to interview

Not whoever answers first. The customers the business wants more of:

- High deal size.
- Short sales cycle (they understood fast; their language converts fast).
- Low churn (they got lasting value).

Ways to find them, best first:

1. Filter the customer list by those three signals. If the Commander has connected a customer database, find its tools with `tool.search`; otherwise ask for an export.
2. Ask the sales and support people who loves the product and can say why.
3. End every call with "Who else should we talk to?"

Aim for ten calls and be content with five. Themes repeat by the fifth or sixth.

## Incentives

The upstream method suggests about $50 for a 30-minute call and $5 for a survey response, offered up front. These cost the Commander money: propose them, never promise them on the Commander's behalf.

## Invitation draft

Short, specific, low commitment. Write it as a draft and hold it for the Commander.

```
Subject: Quick favour, 30 minutes

Hi [first name],

I'm [name] from [company]. I'm trying to get better at helping customers
like you, and I'd love 30 minutes to hear how [product area] is really
working for you: what's good, what's annoying, what you wish were
different. No pitch, no agenda.

[Thank-you offer, if the Commander approved one.]

Are you free [day] or [day] this week?

Thanks either way,
[Name]
```

One clear ask, two concrete times. "No pitch" and "what's annoying" signal that the truth is wanted. Never call it a research study.

## Keep asking why

The first answer is rarely the real one. Follow each answer down three to five levels until you reach the root motive, the business result or the feeling behind it.

Example, following a downgrade:

- Why did you downgrade? "We weren't using the advanced reports."
- Why not? "Nobody on the team knew how to build one."
- Why didn't anyone learn? "The person who set us up left, and new people never got the training."
- Why did that lead to a downgrade? "Without the reports my boss couldn't see the return, so at renewal it looked like an easy cut."

The surface answer is "we don't use reports". The root is a training gap that turns a customer who could have grown into one who shrinks. Nobody can fix "they don't use reports". Retraining new people and showing the return to the buyer can be fixed.

Ask about both pains (what is broken, what they would pay to escape) and passions (what they love and would hate to lose). Pains drive buying. Passions drive staying and referring.

## The fit survey (Sean Ellis)

The most useful single survey question:

> How would you feel if you could no longer use [product]?
> Very disappointed / Somewhat disappointed / Not disappointed / I no longer use it

Benchmark: when 40 percent or more answer "very disappointed", the product likely has strong fit. Below that, keep improving. Superhuman famously steered its roadmap by this number and reported reaching 58 percent.

Follow with:

- "What type of person would benefit most from [product]?" (sharpens the target customer)
- "What is the main benefit you get?" (positioning in their words)
- "How can we improve it for you?" (roadmap, especially from the "somewhat" group)

Split every answer by the "very disappointed" group against the rest. That group is the true market. Run the survey repeatedly, not once.

## Survey guardrails

- Short. Every extra question loses completions.
- Open questions for language; multiple-choice answers only reflect the options given.
- No leading questions.

## Afterwards

Ask the Commander for the notes or transcript text (there is no audio transcription) and run it through the extraction framework and confidence labels in the main skill. First-hand answers outrank public posts when the two disagree.
