# Cold email frameworks

Frameworks teach a way of thinking; they are not copy to paste. The sample lines below use square-bracket placeholders. Fill a placeholder only with a fact from your source table or from the Commander. A result or customer name that nobody gave you stays a `[PROOF NEEDED]` marker.

## PAS: problem, agitate, solution (the default)

Name the pain, show what it costs, present the solution with a soft ask.
Best for readers who know the problem but not the solution.

> Most [role] at companies your size spend [time] a week on [task]. That is time not spent on [what matters], and it often means [consequence]. We built [solution in one line]. [Customer] cut [metric] by [amount]. Would it make sense to see how?

## BAB: before, after, bridge

The painful present, the better future, your product as the way across.
Best for offers with a clear before and after.

> Right now your team is likely [current pain]. Picture [better state]. That is what [product] does. [Customer] saw [result] within [period]. Can I show you how?

## QVC: question, value, ask

A pointed question about the pain, one line of value, a direct next step.
Best for senior readers who want brevity.

> Are your [team] spending more time on [low-value task] than [high-value task]? We help [who] [do what]. [Proof point]. Worth a ten-minute look?

## AIDA: attention, interest, desire, action

A hook or a number, their specific challenge, proof, a clear ask.
Best for readers who respond to data. The number must be real and sourced.

## PPP: praise, picture, push

A genuine, specific compliment; how things could be better; a gentle push.
Best for senior readers, and only with a real trigger you read yourself.

> Your talk on [topic] made a point I keep coming back to: [specific point]. What if [better outcome]? [One line on how]. Open to a quick chat?

## Star, story, solution

Introduce a customer, tell their problem, show the result.
Best when the Commander has a strong, real customer story. Do not write a story about a customer who does not exist.

## SCQ: situation, complication, question

Their present reality, the thing complicating it, a question that speaks to the need.
Best for consultative selling.

> Your team doubled this year. That usually means onboarding eats into selling time. How are you handling ramp-up for new hires?

## ACCA: awareness, comprehension, conviction, action

A contrarian opening, the benefit in plain terms, proof, a firm ask.
Best for analytical buyers who need evidence: engineers, finance, operations.

## 3C: compliment, case study, ask (Alex Berman)

Best for agencies and services. The case study carries the email.

> Big fan of [specific thing about the company]. We just built [thing] for [similar company] that [result]. I have a few ideas for you. Interested?

## Mouse Trap (Will Allred, Lavender)

One observation plus one yes-or-no question about value. One or two sentences.
Best for maximum brevity.

> Looks like you're hiring [role]. Would it help to see [specific thing] as they ramp up?

## Justin Michael method

Trigger or pain, a hint of the solution, a yes-or-no ask. One to three sentences, no introduction. Built for phones. For the most important prospects, quote their own published words back to them, exactly and with the source in your table.

## Vanilla Ice Cream (Lavender)

Observation, problem or insight, credibility, solution, invitation to talk. A five-part base that fits almost any situation.

## PASTOR (Ray Edwards)

Problem, amplify, story, testimony, offer, response. Best spread across a sequence of several emails for complex services, one element per email.

## Choosing

| Situation | Reach for |
|---|---|
| No strong signal about the person | PAS, QVC or SCQ at role level |
| A real, recent trigger you read | Mouse Trap, PPP, or trigger-insight-ask |
| A strong real customer story | Star-story-solution, 3C |
| Senior executive | QVC, Mouse Trap, Justin Michael |
| Analytical buyer | ACCA, AIDA with a sourced number |
