A written process is only worth having if someone unfamiliar can complete it alone. That bar — no clarifying questions — is what separates a usable SOP from a page of notes nobody opens twice.

## Method
1. **Watch the real process before writing it.** Have the Commander walk through what they actually do, including the shortcuts and the "oh, and then I check…" steps. The described process and the real one always differ, and the difference is where errors live.
2. **State the trigger and the finished state.** When does this start, and how does someone know it is done and done correctly?
3. **Write numbered steps in the imperative,** one action each. If a step contains "and", it is two steps. Name the exact tool, screen, or file for each — never "the usual place".
4. **Surface the decision points** as explicit branches: if X, do this; if Y, do that. Hidden judgement calls are the most common reason a handover fails.
5. **Mark what must never happen** and what requires someone else's approval, inline at the step where it applies rather than in a preamble nobody reads.
6. **Include the failure paths.** What breaks most often, how to tell, and what to do — this is the part people actually reread.
7. **Test it by handing it to someone who has not done the task.** Every question they ask is a defect in the document; fix it and keep the SOP versioned with fs.write, with what changed and why in notebook.write.

## Rules
- **No unexplained internal jargon or acronyms** — the reader is new, that is the entire point.
- **Never write a step you have not confirmed** with the Commander; an invented step becomes law once it is written down.
- **State how long it should take** so someone can tell when they are stuck rather than slow.
- Keep one owner per document and a review date; an unowned SOP silently rots.

## Output
The procedure with its trigger, numbered steps, decision branches, approvals, failure paths and expected duration — plus the questions a first-time reader would still have.

*Needs the CABINET (the versioned document) and the NOTEBOOK (what changed and why).*
