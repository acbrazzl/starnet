# Pricing models

Eight ways to structure how you charge. This is separate from the value metric (the unit you charge on) and the tier structure (how you package). Most real products combine two or more. The companies named are well-known users of each model; open their pricing page before quoting any current price.

## The eight models

| Model | How it works | Best when | Known for it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Flat rate | One price, one product, everyone pays the same | Simple product, one kind of customer, no pricing friction wanted | Basecamp |
| Usage-based | Pay for what you consume | Value rises directly with volume and volume is easy to meter | Stripe |
| Tiered | Good, better, best packages at set prices | Distinct segments with different needs and budgets | Most self-serve software |
| Per user | A price per seat | Value grows as more people in the company use it | Notion |
| Feature-based | Price unlocks capabilities | Clear feature groups map to willingness to pay | Intercom |
| Credits | Buy a bucket of credits and spend them on actions | Usage is lumpy; prepayment and simple mental accounting are wanted | Audible |
| Outcome-based | Pay per result delivered | The outcome can be measured and clearly attributed, and it is what the buyer wants | AI support agents billed per resolved conversation |
| Hybrid | A deliberate mix, for example platform fee plus usage | One model under-charges some customers and over-charges others | Most mature products |

## When to reach for each

- **Flat rate:** try it first if you can. Easiest to sell, understand and forecast. The cost: money left on the table with the biggest customers.
- **Usage-based:** the fairest when consumption tracks value, but revenue is less predictable and buyers fear a surprise bill. Pair it with spend caps or alerts.
- **Tiered:** the default for self-serve software. One page can serve small and mid-size customers.
- **Per user:** only if value really rises with headcount. If it does not, seats punish adoption and teams share logins.
- **Feature-based:** strong for segmenting. Never gate the feature that delivers the core value; gate the ones that separate casual from serious users.
- **Credits:** good for products where each action has a real cost, such as AI features. Credits separate price from a single unit and make prepaying feel natural.
- **Outcome-based:** the newest model, common with AI agents (per resolved ticket, per completed run). Highest trust because the buyer pays only when they win, but it only works when the outcome is measurable and clearly yours.
- **Hybrid:** where most mature products end up. A base fee for predictability plus a usage or outcome part for upside.

## Combining models

- Tiers plus per user: seats inside each package. Most business software.
- Platform fee plus usage: predictable base, variable upside.
- Seats plus credits: pay per person, top up for heavy actions.
- Feature tiers plus outcome: unlock capabilities by tier, charge per result on top.

Pick the primary model from the value metric. Add a second only if a single model clearly misprices a real segment.

## The value-to-price ratio

Aim for roughly ten to one (a rule attributed to Ryan Kulp): the customer should see about ten times more value than they pay. That margin makes the purchase feel obvious and leaves room to raise prices as value is added. If ten-times value cannot be put into words, fix the offer or the positioning before the price.

## Low prices hurt retention

Charging too little is not the safe choice. Subscription pricing research associated with Patrick Campbell and ProfitWell, echoed by several operators, holds that under-priced customers churn more, because a low price signals low value and attracts the least committed buyers.

Related: customers who negotiate a discount tend to churn at a much higher rate than full-price customers. Discounting to close often buys a customer who leaves anyway.

So, when in doubt, price higher. It is easier to bring a price down for someone than to pull it up.

Treat these as the upstream author's summary of others' findings, not as figures StarNet has verified.
