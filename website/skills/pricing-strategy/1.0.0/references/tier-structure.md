# Tier structure and packaging

## How many tiers

- **Two:** a simple, clear choice. Works for a clean small-business versus enterprise split. Risk: money left on the table.
- **Three:** the standard. Entry, recommended, premium.
- **Four or more:** finer steps for a wide range of customer sizes. Risk: decision paralysis.

## Good, better, best

| Tier | Purpose | Includes | Price | For |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Good | Remove the barrier to starting | Core features, limited usage | Low | Small teams, people trying it out |
| Better | Where most customers land | Full features, reasonable limits | The anchor price | Growing teams, serious users |
| Best | Capture high-value customers | Everything, advanced features, higher limits | Often two to three times Better | Larger teams, power users |

## Ways to separate tiers

- **Feature gating:** basics everywhere, advanced features higher up. Works when features differ clearly in value.
- **Usage limits:** same features, different limits (users, storage, calls). Works when value scales with usage.
- **Support level:** email, then priority, then a dedicated contact. Works for products that take effort to set up.
- **Access and customisation:** API, single sign-on, custom branding. Works for enterprise.

## Packaging by persona

Customers differ in willingness to pay, feature needs, how they buy and how they see value. Segment by company size, use case, sophistication and industry.

Step 1. Define the personas:

| Persona | Size | Needs | Willingness to pay |
|---|---|---|---|
| Freelancer | 1 person | Basic features | Low |
| Small team | 2 to 10 | Collaboration | Medium |
| Growing company | 10 to 50 | Scale, integrations | Higher |
| Enterprise | 50 and up | Security, support | High, custom |

Step 2. Map features to personas: core features for all; collaboration from small team up; full integrations and API from growing company up; single sign-on, audit logs and a custom contract for enterprise.

Step 3. Price to value for each persona. Set prices that capture value without blocking adoption. Evidence for each persona's willingness to pay comes from the Commander's customers, not from this table.

## Free plan or free trial

**A free plan works when** the product spreads by use, free users add value (content, data, referrals), the market is large enough that a small conversion rate pays, serving free users costs little, and there is a clear limit that triggers the upgrade.
Risks: free users who never convert, a cheaper image, support cost, harder price rises later.

**A free trial works when** the product needs time to show value, setup takes effort, several people decide, the price is higher, and the product is sticky once configured.
Practice: 7 to 14 days for simple products, 14 to 30 for complex ones; full access; clear countdown and reminders.
Asking for a card up front raises the share of trials that convert and lowers the number of trials. Which matters more is the Commander's call.

**Mixes:** a free plan plus a trial of the paid features; or a reverse trial, where new users start with everything and drop to the free plan when the trial ends.

## Enterprise pricing

Add "Contact sales" when deals exceed about $10,000 a year, customers need custom contracts, setup help is required, or security reviews and procurement are involved.

- **Expected in the tier:** single sign-on, audit logs, admin controls, an uptime commitment, security certifications.
- **Extras:** dedicated support, custom onboarding, training, custom integrations, a voice in the roadmap.
- **Pricing approaches:** per seat with volume discounts; a platform fee plus usage above a threshold; contracts tied to the customer's results (a share of transactions or revenue).

Even with an enterprise tier, show at least a starting price somewhere on the page. A page where every tier says "Contact us" loses both human buyers and AI assistants to a competitor that shows numbers.
